N2 MODULAR AIRCRAFT HARDPOINT
Status as of October 28, 2026 (Eastern): selected development mechanism; no physical load or release qualification.

PURPOSE AND CURRENT SCOPE

The hardpoint started as a removable aircraft attachment for care packages, small gliders, sensors/cameras, and external batteries. That original brief combined mechanical retention, release, and electrical power/data transfer, with an approximately 10–15 lb carried-mass target. Several iterations deliberately simplified it.

The active design is now N2, the supported cross-bolt. You selected it after the six-mechanism comparison and the corrected extraction-direction FEA. The selected P11 package makes N2 the sole active mechanism and archives the alternatives. P11 consolidates the existing N2 design; it does not contain a newly strengthened geometry or new physical tests.

Two scope changes are essential. The 10–15 lb requirement was explicitly withdrawn during the compact redesign. The current N2 assembly is mechanical retention and release only: it has no payload power/data contacts, aircraft-wide electrical architecture, or independent positive lock for a permanently mounted pod. Those original capabilities remain future engineering work if you bring them back. Disconnecting the solenoid does not create a positive fixed-pod lock.

CURRENT MECHANISM

The nominal retained envelope is 43.1 × 30 × 19 mm. It excludes full wiring, remote electronics, aircraft/payload attachment hardware, backing, and departure clearance. Four pieces are printed: receiver, payload adapter, keeper, and solenoid clamp. The actuator is one complete Adafruit 2776 mini spring-return solenoid. Two M2 × 16 screws and two M2 nuts retain its clamp; these are not the airframe mounting screws.

The 3 × 3 mm printed keeper crosses the adapter's 1.4 mm lug. In the retained state, the receiver supports the keeper on both sides of that lug. The solenoid pulls the keeper in +X. Once unlocked, the payload adapter departs in +Z under gravity or another external force. There is no ejector or departure sensor. The animated website shows prescribed motion, not measured release dynamics.

The existing CAD gives this stroke accounting:

Distal support engagement = 0.6 mm.
Travel needed to clear the lug = 2.3 mm.
Nominal solenoid stroke = 3.0 mm.
Full-open clearance = 3.0 - 2.3 = 0.7 mm.
Retained travel after losing distal support = 2.3 - 0.6 = 1.7 mm.

That last interval is the main engineering problem. N2 gains strength from support on both sides while closed, but the far support disappears before the lug is free. The remaining guide must carry the load during part of the withdrawal. Printed roughness, skew, flexure, and friction can then make the keeper bind even if the fully retained assembly holds the load.

WHAT THE ANALYSIS ACTUALLY SAYS

The PLA study used the same effective material assumptions across mechanisms: elastic modulus 2,200 MPa, Poisson ratio 0.35, solid-equivalent prints at 22°C, and assumed tension/compression/shear thresholds of 25/40/15 MPa. It solved linear elasticity with prescribed contact forces. It did not model actual printed layer fracture, creep, nonlinear collapse, realistic bolt preload, or the complete changing release contact.

For N2, the recorded local stress-threshold crossings are:

- 21.50 N with continuous rigid underside backing.
- 11.26 N with an idealized two-hole receiver fixture.
- 7.48–7.53 N with ineffective distal support.

The different values describe different support assumptions; they are not a safe range of payload ratings. Raw stress peaks remain mesh-sensitive. Two bolts do not reproduce the continuously fixed underside. Even a valid holding result would not show that the solenoid can release that load.

For scale only, static mass equivalents are calculated as m = F / 9.80665. Thus 21.50 N corresponds to 2.19 kg at 1g, and 11.26 N corresponds to 1.15 kg at 1g, rounded. These are mathematical conversions of model thresholds, not approved payloads. Departing mass always includes the adapter and its attachment hardware.

Your N4 correction was justified: the earlier comparison loaded the T-rail across its lips, while its adapter actually withdraws along the rail. The corrected extraction model reduced N4's reference screen to 2.47 N. That removed the basis for calling N4 the strongest extraction lock. It did not make N2 universally strongest: fixture choice still changes the ranking, and N3 slightly exceeds N2 in the two-hole comparison. N2 is the selected development choice.

The separate historical release-force calculation estimated peak withdrawal demand near 0.650 * P, increasing to 0.784 * P in one adverse contact assumption, for friction coefficient 0.20. Those coefficients are not measured PLA friction and were not generated by the same coupled contact simulation as the FEA. They identify a question to measure, not a releasable load.

BUILD MATERIALS AND CONTROLS

The current manufacturing basis is PLA. Historical PETG wording remains in preserved CAD manifests, but the active metadata and printing instructions supersede it. The ideal solid-volume print mass totals 9.429 g. Adding the supplier-listed 12.6 g solenoid gives a partial 22.029 g mechanism estimate, before screws, wiring, controller, and installation hardware. The adapter's 0.644 g estimate belongs in the departing-mass account.

The local bench controls use a Pico H, Adafruit 5648 driver, ARM/FIRE buttons, and a regulated 5 V supply. Firmware, wiring, software tests, and blank measurement records are included. The initial 250 ms command pulse and ten-second lockout are development settings; no Pico was flashed and no physical GPIO, current, or release test was demonstrated for this consolidation.

The historical October 26 BOM gives a $5.99 consumed mechanism subtotal and a $15.19 known priced subset including remote controls and prorated switches, or $17.44 using the full example switch pack. Those omit unpriced wiring, equipment, installation hardware, other pack minima, shipping, tax, labor, and qualification. They are neither refreshed prices nor a complete installed cost. No custom PCB or PCBWay quote exists.

REALISTIC NEXT STEP

Make one instrumented bench prototype. First print the four parts and measure the purchased actuator, actual distal overlap, full-open clearance, and repeatable unloaded return. Do not proceed to loaded trials if the keeper binds, loses retained distal engagement within its play, or cannot clear the lug. The existing plan's provisional minimum open clearance is 0.20 mm after measured variation.

Then measure both available net solenoid pull and mechanism withdrawal demand across the entire stroke. The plan proposes a 2:1 measured force margin before progressing. Use a contained fixture to increase load gradually and separately record command, actual unlock, and complete departure. Test warm prints, repeated cycles, partial-power/recovery behavior, and the intended mounting/backing. Aircraft installation and separation come after these gates. The next useful evidence is a measured release, not another favorable retained-state stress image.
